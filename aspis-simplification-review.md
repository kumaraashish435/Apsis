# ASPIS Roadmap — Self-Critique: Where the Original Plan Over-Engineers

A challenge was raised against the ASPIS five-product roadmap (`aspis-blueprint.md`): identify every place where the plan over-engineers or introduces technology too early, and recommend simpler alternatives that still support the long-term vision — favoring a 3-month path over a 12-month path wherever a feature can be safely delayed.

## The meta-pattern

Almost every "NEW" infrastructure item in the original roadmap was justified with some version of *"do this now so you never rewrite it later."* That's backwards for a 2-person team. The real cost comparison is: **unused infrastructure sitting idle for 2–3 product cycles** vs. **the actual cost of introducing it later, which is usually smaller than assumed.** A modular monolith with clean internal boundaries is *cheap* to split later. A distributed system built before it's needed is *expensive* to operate every single day until it finally is.

## Where the plan over-engineers, item by item

| # | Where | What the plan did | Why it's premature | Simpler version | Revisit when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Product 1** | Stood up a separate C++ engine + gRPC service just for SGP4 propagation | Mature SGP4 ports already exist in C#/.NET. Standing up a second language, a build toolchain, cross-process serialization, and a deployment unit — for one library call — is the biggest time sink in Sprint 1–2, and it buys nothing until a *later* product needs it | Call a validated SGP4 library **in-process, in C#**. Keep the same golden-file test discipline against Vallado's vectors — that part was right — just no gRPC boundary yet | When real subsystem physics (Product 4) or genuine performance/multi-language needs (Python AI touching sim internals) make a separate engine process actually pay for itself |
| 2 | **Product 1/2** | gRPC contracts between services that had exactly one caller | Protobuf codegen, versioning discipline, and a network boundary are overhead with no second consumer yet | Plain in-process function calls / a normal internal API | The moment a second, independently-deployed service genuinely needs to call it |
| 3 | **Product 1** | Propagator comparison + 3 export formats in the MVP | These are differentiators, not "does the core loop work" — they compete with sprints that prove whether anyone wants the product at all | Ship "create satellite → see it orbit → save/share" first. Add comparison and extra export formats as a fast-follow once real users are pulling for it | Post-launch, driven by actual user requests |
| 4 | **Product 2** | Full 3-tier RBAC (Viewer/Editor/Admin) enforced everywhere | Team hasn't validated that teams even want shared missions yet, and granular roles are something customers ask for only once they have >5 users — none of the early customers will | One role: "member." Everyone in an org can do everything. Add granular roles when a paying customer actually asks | First customer with a real internal permissions conflict |
| 5 | **Product 2** | Gantt-style Mission Timeline | Gantt rendering (custom or library) is real frontend effort for a visualization that isn't the core value | A sortable table of events/passes first | Once the underlying data model is stable and the table view feels genuinely limiting |
| 6 | **Product 3** | RabbitMQ as the event/command bus | One simulation producer, one API consumer. A message broker earns its keep with decoupling, retries, and multiple independent producers/consumers — not before | An in-process background worker (`BackgroundService` in ASP.NET Core) ticks the sim, writes to Postgres, pushes via SignalR directly — no broker in between | Multiple concurrent simulation workers, or Command Dispatch genuinely needs guaranteed delivery/retry semantics at scale |
| 7 | **Product 3** | Full event sourcing as the *sole* source of truth (fold-over-log for current state) | A real distributed-systems commitment — snapshot management, "what's the battery level right now" becomes a fold operation, schema versioning discipline. Doing this properly is a multi-month investment, not a 2-week sprint line item — it was underpriced | A **mutable current-state table** (easy to query) **plus** an append-only audit-log table for history. Gets 80% of replay/audit value at a fraction of the engineering cost | Product 4, when Replay/Failure-Injection/Scenario-Comparison genuinely need true fold-over-log semantics |
| 8 | **Product 3** | TimescaleDB from day one | Hypertables and continuous aggregates solve a data-volume problem that doesn't exist yet — one simulated mission, a handful of channels, a few users | A plain Postgres table with a `(channel_id, time)` index. Same schema; add the Timescale extension later — a low-friction migration when actually needed | Real fleet-scale volume or long retention windows (Product 4/5 territory) |
| 9 | **Product 3** | Redis SignalR backplane | Backplanes solve multi-instance scale-out. A single API instance doesn't need one | Plain SignalR, single instance | The day more than one API instance is actually running |
| 10 | **Product 3** | AKS/Kubernetes migration | The biggest one. A production-grade k8s setup — secrets, ingress, autoscaling, monitoring, on-call runbooks — done by two people learning as they go is realistically a **2–3 month tax**, not the one-sprint line item originally given | **Azure Container Apps** (or just scaled App Service instances) — container orchestration and autoscaling without operating a cluster | A specific, measured trigger: concurrent simulation sessions that Container Apps' scale-to-N genuinely can't handle |
| 11 | **Product 4** | Extracting `TwinOrchestrator` into its own deployable service | Justified by "the boundary is stable," but stability alone isn't a reason to split — this broke the plan's own "microservices only when justified" principle | Keep it a module in the monolith | An actual operational forcing function: independent scaling needs, independent deploy cadence, or a language change |
| 12 | **Product 4** | Exact deterministic re-simulation replay, specified directly in Sprint 9–10 | Depends on the full event-sourcing infra from #7, plus serious determinism testing — genuinely hard, multi-month-grade work if done right. This also contradicted earlier advice (from an even earlier draft) to build simple frame-playback first | Replay = **re-display already-computed telemetry frames** from Postgres. Instant, simple, good enough for a UI scrubber | Once a customer needs "replay with a modified input" — a real, specific ask, not a hypothetical one |
| 13 | **Product 4** | Scenario Comparison bundled into the initial Digital Twin release | Running concurrent forked simulations + a diff UI is real added complexity on top of Replay + Failure Injection, which are the actual core value | Ship Replay + Failure Injection alone first; add Comparison as a fast-follow | Once Replay/Failure Injection have proven customer pull |
| 14 | **Product 5** | Treating all 7 AI features as one 14-week bundle at equal effort | LLM-based features (Assistant, NL Query, RCA narration) were priced the same as from-scratch ML (anomaly detection v2, predictive-maintenance LSTM) — but the LLM ones are "call an API + RAG over data already on hand" (2–3 weeks), while real model training is a genuinely longer, separately-paced investment | Ship the **LLM-based features first** (Assistant, NL Query) — fast, visible "AI" value. Treat ML-from-scratch (anomaly v2, predictive maintenance) as its own longer-horizon track that doesn't gate the AI launch | Decouple the two tracks; don't let model-training timelines delay shipping the assistant |

## What should NOT be cut

This isn't "cut everything." Some things are cheap and non-negotiable regardless of team size:

- **Golden-file physics validation** (running through every product) — it's how you know a propagator is actually correct, and it doesn't get simpler by deferring it.
- **Secrets management from day one** (Key Vault) — low-cost insurance, not premature.
- **The discipline** around command authorization (auth checks, an audit trail) — what's being cut is the *heavyweight infrastructure underneath it* (RabbitMQ, full event sourcing), not the discipline itself.
- **The event schema design conversation** in Product 3, done jointly and early — just store it more simply (current-state table + audit log) than a true event-sourced log until Product 4 needs the real thing.

## What this buys you

| Product | Original estimate | Simplified estimate | Saved |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Orbital Simulator | 12 weeks | ~8 weeks | 4 weeks |
| 2 — Mission Planner | 10 weeks | ~8 weeks | 2 weeks |
| 3 — Mission Control | 12 weeks | ~8 weeks (plus avoids an unmeasured multi-month k8s tax) | 4+ weeks |
| 4 — Digital Twin | 12 weeks | ~9 weeks | 3 weeks |

That's roughly **46 weeks down to 33** on the sprint count alone for Products 1–4 — call it 8 months instead of 11 — and that's *before* counting the Kubernetes learning-curve tax that was badly underpriced originally, which is where a "3 months vs. 12 months" gap most plausibly hides in this roadmap. The single highest-leverage cut is #10 (skip Kubernetes, use Container Apps), followed by #6/#7 (skip RabbitMQ and full event sourcing) — those three together remove most of the distributed-systems tax a 2-person team would otherwise pay for scale it doesn't have customers for yet.
